# Summary: Enterprise technology governance (Valentine, 2016)

Descriptive summary of what the thesis says. It does not apply the archive's lenses (EPISTEMOLOGY.md §5). Unless stated otherwise, claims are the thesis's own, with page references.

## Question and purpose

The thesis addresses a gap it describes between boards knowing technology is important and boards building the capability to govern it, a "knowing–doing gap" (PDF pp. 24–25). It states that no board-relevant competency set for enterprise technology governance (ETG) existed when the study began (PDF p. 24).

Three objectives (PDF p. 26):

1. Confirm the extent to which ETG competencies are considered necessary.
2. Provide a validated, flexible set of generic ETG competencies for board directors.
3. Indicate competency priorities for different industry sectors.

Four research questions (PDF p. 90): RQ1, to what extent ETG competencies are deemed necessary; RQ2, what generic competencies are required; RQ3, how perceived priorities vary between industries; RQ4, to what extent the derived competencies are deemed important and fit for purpose by industry.

The thesis defines ETG as supporting the board and senior executives in fulfilling their duty of care. In its definition, ETG is part of board leadership and governance: it aligns and oversees enterprise information and technology with strategy, structure, systems, policies and governance processes; it fosters data-driven decision making and minimises risk; and it creates value through stakeholder engagement and strategic investment (PDF p. 23; a variant appears on PDF pp. 43–44).

It defines digital leadership as the organisation demonstrating technology maturity, IT governance maturity and digital change leadership (PDF p. 17).

## Method and evidence

- **Design:** applied research with a two-phase, exploratory mixed-methods design, run in parallel with a seven-step competency development process the author designed for this study (PDF pp. 92, 98–100, 117–122).
- **Guiding lens:** Strategic Job Modelling (Schippmann, 2013), used throughout to keep the focus on the director's role (PDF pp. 28, 101–103).
- **Rigor assessment:** the author self-assessed process rigor against a modified version of Schippmann et al.'s (2000) 10-point rigor scale, rating it "medium to high" after Phase 1 and "high to very high" after Phase 2 (PDF pp. 103–105, 180–182, 216–219).
- **Straw-man set:** built from about 250 industry and academic documents, coded under four enterprise architecture headings (PDF pp. 78–85).
- **Phase 1:**
  - A single "revelatory" case study in an anonymised ASX 100 company, with six structured interviews: four executives and two parent-company board members (PDF pp. 106–108, 138–153).
  - A "temperature check" online survey with 93 responses, 86 of them complete. About 70% of respondents were from Australia and New Zealand; 53% had board experience (PDF pp. 156–160).
- **Phase 2:** an online survey with 199 participants, of whom 177 completed the ranking task. Participants rank-ordered the behavioural descriptors under each competency and rated the importance of each competency (PDF pp. 185–191).
- **Analysis:** Kruskal–Wallis and Mann–Whitney U tests on the rankings, by industry cluster, role, organisation type and public/private sector (PDF pp. 131–132, 191–200).
- **Sampling:** snowball sampling plus "Social Media Sampling", an approach the author developed using a research website, LinkedIn governance groups, Twitter and Facebook (PDF pp. 114–127).
- **Participant count:** 419 in total: 6 case-study participants, 93 in survey 1, 177 in survey 2, and 142 social-network participants (PDF pp. 187, 225).

## Main findings

- **Need (RQ1):** 74.42% of survey-1 respondents agreed (19.77%) or strongly agreed (54.65%) that it is now very important for boards to include directors with IT governance knowledge, skills and experience. 15.11% disagreed or strongly disagreed (PDF pp. 161, 232).
- **Who disagreed:** of the 13 who disagreed, 10 were born between 1940 and 1959 and 11 had no IT-related education (PDF pp. 161–162, 233).
- **The competency set (RQ2):** one overarching competency, "Enterprise Technology Governance", with three sub-competencies. Each has a definition, a capability statement and behavioural descriptors. The final titles are (PDF pp. 211–215):
  - C1: Direct and govern information and technology-enabled strategy.
  - C2: Lead and govern business information, technology risk and compliance.
  - C3: Direct and govern information and technology-enabled innovation and value creation.
- **How the set changed:** after feedback from case-study participants, the sub-competency headings moved from enterprise architecture categories to board-role headings (PDF pp. 150–152, 179). The final revision added "information" to most descriptors and cut the descriptors in C2 and C3 (PDF pp. 209–210). The thesis states that all iterations of the set are the author's copyright (PDF p. 170).
- **Industry variation (RQ3):** no significant differences were found in descriptor rankings as a function of industry cluster, company size or qualification (PDF pp. 191, 193, 200). A few differences were reported by role (C1 and C3 descriptors) and by organisation type and public/private sector (one C2 descriptor) (PDF pp. 194–200).
- **Fitness for purpose (RQ4):** the three competencies received mean importance ratings of 4.63, 4.47 and 4.25 out of 5, with an overall mean of 4.45. On average, 90.40% of participants rated them somewhat or very important (PDF p. 201). The thesis treats the absence of industry variation plus these ratings as evidence that the set is "fit for purpose" (PDF pp. 200, 219–220, 239–240).
- **Governance by exception:** a concept introduced from a case-study participant's phrase. It describes boards relying on exception reporting through board papers at monthly or quarterly meetings. The thesis argues this may be too slow for technology risk, and proposes parallel rapid-response risk mechanisms alongside it (PDF pp. 147–150, 236).
- **Five "significant insights"** from the literature review (PDF pp. 53, 54, 58, 63, 64; restated on pp. 86–88):
  - A competent board can both ask and critically review.
  - ETG has become a core director competency, level with legal and financial.
  - Traditional governance mechanisms may be too slow.
  - Without a competency set, boards lack direction for recruitment and development.
  - Without competency, board judgement on technology is impaired.
- **Six factors** said to make ETG a core board capability (PDF pp. 243–244):
  - Converging SMACT technologies.
  - Increased scrutiny of boards.
  - IT governance becoming integral to corporate governance.
  - The persistence of governance by exception.
  - Failures of once-iconic brands.
  - ETG being part of fiduciary duty.

## Stated limitations

As stated in the thesis:

- External validity (generalisability) could not be established (PDF p. 96).
- Internal validity in the causal sense was not possible because the study was exploratory (PDF pp. 96, 224).
- The study cannot be repeated with the same participants (PDF pp. 96, 202, 224).
- Snowball and social media sampling are non-probability convenience methods, so representativeness is hard to judge (PDF pp. 115, 248–249).
- Survey 1 may be biased towards technology-inclined respondents (PDF pp. 160, 162, 233).
- Sample sizes were small in 20 of 25 industry sectors (PDF p. 202).
- The case organisation required full anonymity (PDF pp. 108, 248).
- No other ETG competency set existed for comparison (PDF p. 248).
- Improvement in board capability from using the set could not be tested longitudinally (PDF p. 92).

## Reading notes for curator review

These are generated observations made while reading. They are not part of the descriptive summary and are not judgements of standing.

1. **What "validation" means in this thesis.** Validation here rests on practitioners' perceived need, ratings of importance, and rankings that did not vary across industries, gathered from a convenience sample (PDF pp. 200–201, 239–240). The thesis itself says external validity and causal internal validity were not established (PDF pp. 96, 224). This matters for any future tier decision on the Valentine ETG lens (CR-0006).
2. **Reported significance values.** Several results are described as significant with reported values of p = 0.46, 0.18, p < 0.46 and p < 0.45 (PDF pp. 195, 199–200, 230). These are above conventional thresholds as printed. They may be typographical (for example, 0.046). The thesis text does not resolve this.
3. **Inconsistent C2 title.** Table 5.19 gives the final C2 title as "Lead and govern business information, technology risk and compliance" (PDF p. 213). The text elsewhere gives "Lead and govern business technology investment and risk" (PDF pp. 219, 229, 233).
4. **Number of research questions.** The literature review says the observations "motivate three research questions" and then lists four (PDF p. 90).
5. **Absence-based claims.** The claims of being "first known" (PDF pp. 3, 28, 223, 245) rest on the author's searches not finding a prior set. That is a bounded search result, not proof that none existed (named test: *lack of evidence is not evidence of lack*).
